# Human factors

Exercise Wharf Lantern is a decisions exercise under a clock. The technical facts are thin on purpose. What usually decides the hour is attention, status, and whether people can say "I do not know" without being punished for it.

This note is a working brief, not a research paper. It does not report findings from a sitting, because the sitting has not been written up here. Mosa Mofokeng's background is in Industrial and Organisational Psychology, and this is the part of the kit that background is meant to strengthen. Extend it in your own words after you have facilitated. Do not add percentages, "studies show" lines, or participant quotes you do not have.

The public relations half of the same idea lives in [comms/crisis-communications-plan.md](comms/crisis-communications-plan.md). A frightened room writes frightened notices. A room that cannot disagree with the most senior person writes the sentence that person said first.

## What stress does to a decision

In the first minutes of inject 1, people narrow. They lock onto the photo, the note, or the wish to look competent, and they drop the question of who else already saw the photo. That is ordinary acute stress. Working memory gets smaller. The room reaches for a familiar action: reboot, wipe, reassure, call a meeting. Several of those actions destroy the very record the firm will need.

You will also see these patterns. Name them when you see them. Naming them is not an insult.

- **Normalcy bias.** "It is probably a glitch, let the 09:00 meeting run." The brief tempts this. Amina says it out loud in inject 2.
- **Confirmation bias.** The Thursday attachment becomes the whole story, and later facts get bent to fit it. The injects mark that attachment as unproven for this reason.
- **Authority gradient.** A junior person knows the Stellenbosch laptop should come off the network and does not say so because the managing director wants calm. The gradient is steeper when the board chair is waiting for a soothing call.
- **Groupthink.** The first confident payment opinion gets nods, and the scribe never records the quiet objection. Ask the quiet person before you close inject 4.
- **Action bias.** Doing something visible, including announcing "no data was stolen", feels better than holding an incomplete sentence. A bad client notice is often this urge written down.

A useful counter is a forced split in every inject: what we know, what we suspect, what we are refusing to do yet. The scribe log has those columns so the split survives the conversation.

## Roles, and why the scribe is a protected role

| Role | Human job, besides the task | Failure mode to watch |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Incident lead | Make the choice and wear it | Soothing the room instead of choosing |
| Scribe | Keep a record people can trust an hour later | Being treated as the minute-taker who may not speak, or as the person who will be blamed for a bad decision they only wrote down |
| Information Officer | Slow the room down when a sentence creates a legal duty | Being talked over because "legal" sounds like delay |
| Technical lead | Say what is confirmed | Disappearing into tools and returning with a theory stated as a fact |
| Communications lead | Hold the wording to the facts | Polishing a sentence until it hides the gap |
| Client services | Keep a real person's deadline in the room | Promising that person something the system cannot do |
| BaySwitch | Represent the operator, including the awkward contract pause | Becoming a villain the room can blame so it does not have to decide |

The scribe may stop the room and say "I do not have a decision yet". That interruption is part of the design. Mosa has sat as a scribe in a ransomware workshop. Use that experience when you brief the scribe: write what was decided, write who objected, and do not tidy the story into what should have been decided. The after action report is allowed to judge. The log is not.

If one person holds two roles, they announce the hat before they speak. Doubling the incident lead and the scribe is the combination to avoid. The person who owns the decision should not be the only record of the decision.

## Psychological safety in a grim scenario

Psychological safety, in Amy Edmondson's sense, is a shared belief that the group will not embarrass, punish, or reject someone for speaking up with a question, a concern, or a mistake. You do not get it by announcing it. You get it by what the facilitator does the first time someone says something unpopular or admits a miss.

In this exercise that means:

- The first dissent is written down and thanked, even if the lead does not adopt it.
- Nobody is asked to play a traumatised client or a shouting journalist. The journalist inject is an e-mail, not a performance.
- "I do not know" is an acceptable technical answer. BaySwitch's script is full of unknowns so the room can practise hearing them.
- Blame directed at a named character ("the adviser who opened the attachment") gets parked. The hot wash can talk about the control that failed. It does not need a culprit to finish.
- Anyone may call a pause. The facilitator guide says how to handle distress. Read it before the day. A tabletop is still a story, and stories about money, identity numbers, and children on beneficiary forms can land on a real memory.

Close the sitting with a check that is not a technique question: is anyone leaving worried about a real incident at work or at home. Offer a conversation outside the group. You are the facilitator, not their therapist, and you do not invent a support service in the notes. If the host organisation has a support route, use that route.

## How this shows up in the communications

Read the draft customer notice against the mood of the room.

- If the room is ashamed, the draft will understate. Look for "no evidence of data loss" standing in for "we have not looked yet".
- If the room is angry, the draft will overclaim. Look for a named attacker, a promise that publication will not happen, or a swipe at BaySwitch.
- If the room is exhausted, the draft will be late and vague. A late notice can still be accurate. A vague notice cannot be repaired by adjectives.

The internal message in the communications plan includes a line that staff may step out. Leave that line in. It is a control, in the same way an offline backup is a control. It keeps a person functioning for the rest of the sitting, and it is the sentence you would want in a real staff note.

## What to capture for the portfolio

After the sitting, write a short account in the after action report under human factors. Useful prompts, still without invented colour:

- At which inject did the room narrow onto one story?
- Who spoke least, and were they invited in?
- Did the scribe interrupt, and what changed after that?
- Which decision would have been different if the most senior person had spoken second?
- What will you change in the next facilitation?

Leave the prompts blank until the room has sat. A rehearsal you did alone can be noted as a rehearsal. It is not participant feedback.
